When does opportunity cost change?

Changes in opportunity cost | Textbook Section 1.3C (p.15–21): five situations, one question

🎙️ — Don't memorise the table yet. The summary table on p.21 has more than ten rows. If you memorise it, you will mix it up. Today we use five real events to understand one idea: cost is what you give up to get your choice. Remember this, and you can build the whole table yourself.

Situation 1

The value of the chosen option changes

StoryThe night 40,000 people shouted "refund"

4 February 2024, Hong Kong Stadium. Inter Miami came to Hong Kong on its pre-season tour to play a team of Hong Kong league players. About 40,000 tickets sold out fast. The cheapest ticket was HK$880. The most expensive was almost HK$5,000. Most people bought tickets to see Lionel Messi.

Messi had a hamstring injury. He sat on the bench for all 90 minutes. Luis Suárez did not play either. At the end, fans shouted "refund", and David Beckham was booed when he thanked the crowd. The government said it was very disappointed. The organiser, Tatler Asia, withdrew its application for HK$16 million of government funding. Later it offered a 50% refund to fans who bought tickets through official channels.

A fan already has a ticket and is sitting in the stadium. Because Messi does not play, "watching this match" is worth much less to the fan. What happens to the opportunity cost of watching the match?

It stays the same. To watch the match, the fan gave up two things: the ticket money, and the best other use of that evening (for example, a part-time job). Whether Messi plays or not changes what the fan gets, not what the fan gives up. The value of the chosen option is the benefit, not the cost.

So why did so many fans feel they "lost"? Because the benefit fell below the cost. If they had known, they would have chosen the job instead. Feeling that you lost comes from comparing cost and benefit. It does not mean the cost went up.

Exam-precise rule

A change in the value of the chosen option will not affect the opportunity cost of choosing it, because the value of the highest-valued option forgone is not affected. It only affects the benefit of the choice, which matters in cost-benefit analysis.

In plain words: if your choice becomes better or worse, the best thing you gave up has not changed. So the cost stays the same. Only the benefit changes.

Common mistake: "If the chosen option falls below the second choice, the cost changes." Wrong. Even if watching the match falls to $300, below the $500 job, the cost is still $500. The only change is that the cost is now bigger than the benefit, so a rational person would not choose it again.

Now look at it another way. Tatler later gave a 50% refund. For a fan who gets the refund, what happens to the cost of having watched the match?

It decreases. The refund does not change the value of watching the match. It reduces the money the fan paid for it. Money cost goes down, so the full cost goes down. This is not Situation 1 anymore. It is Situation 2. One news story, two different economics questions.

Think further: reports said fans had to agree not to take legal action in order to get the refund. Does "accepting the refund" have its own opportunity cost? What does the fan give up?

Situation 2

The money or time spent on the chosen option changes

Story AFive cents and a riot: the 1966 Star Ferry fare rise

The Cross-Harbour Tunnel did not open until 1972. In the 1960s, most people crossed the harbour by ferry. From 1946, the Star Ferry first-class fare was 20 cents, and the second-class fare was 10 cents. In April 1966, the government approved a rise in the first-class fare to 25 cents. The second-class fare stayed the same.

A young man called So Sau-chung went on a hunger strike at the Star Ferry pier to protest. He was arrested the next day. Riots then broke out in Kowloon for several nights, and the government ordered a curfew. Historians generally say the fare rise was only the spark. People were also angry about poverty, corruption and colonial rule. So do not say "five cents made people riot". That is too simple.

A clerk takes the Star Ferry first class to work every day. The boat and the journey time are the same as before. What happens to the opportunity cost of taking first class?

It increases. The same service means the value (benefit) has not changed. But to choose first class, the clerk must now give up 5 more cents, which could have been used for something else. Money cost goes up, so full cost goes up.

The fares are the real prices. The size of the time cost is different for each person, so the bar width is only an example.

Think further: when the cost goes up, what do some people do? They switch to second class. The higher cost means the benefit is no longer bigger than the cost for some people. This is cost-benefit analysis in real life.

Story BThe 2022 World Cup final: extra time and penalties

18 December 2022, Lusail Stadium, Qatar. Argentina played France. The match started at 11 p.m. Hong Kong time. It was 2–2 after 90 minutes and 3–3 after extra time. Argentina won 4–2 on penalties. Many fans in Hong Kong expected to sleep before 1 a.m., but they watched until after 2 a.m.

A Hong Kong student stays up to watch the final. The student loves the extra time and thinks it was worth it. Because of extra time, what happens to the opportunity cost of watching the final?

It increases. The student spends more time on this choice. That time had a best other use (for example, sleeping before a test the next morning). More time means a higher time cost, so a higher opportunity cost. Enjoying it is about the benefit. Just like Situation 1, do not mix the two up.

Bar widths are only examples.

Exam-precise rule

When the expenses for the chosen option increase (decrease), the money cost and hence the opportunity cost of choosing it increase (decrease). When the time spent on the chosen option increases (decreases), the time cost and hence the opportunity cost increase (decrease).

In plain words: if you must pay more money or spend more time to get your choice, you give up more, so the cost goes up. Time has a cost because you could use it for something else.

Situation 3

The value of the highest-valued option forgone changes

Story1849: San Francisco Bay full of empty ships

In January 1848, James Marshall found gold at Sutter's Mill in California. When the news spread, huge numbers of people rushed to California in 1849. This is called the Gold Rush.

Something strange happened in San Francisco. When ships sailed into the bay, their sailors left the ships and went to look for gold. Hundreds of empty ships sat in the bay. Records from that time say many shops closed and workers quit their jobs to go to the goldfields.

A sailor's job had not become worse. The pay had not fallen. So why did so many sailors stop working?

A sailor arrives in San Francisco in 1849. His best other option is "going to find gold". The expected reward from gold-digging rises a lot. What happens to the opportunity cost of "staying on the ship as a sailor"?

It increases. Opportunity cost is the value of the highest-valued option forgone. When the best option given up becomes more valuable, the cost goes up with it. When the cost becomes bigger than the benefit of being a sailor, a rational sailor leaves the ship. A job with exactly the same pay can become very costly because of changes outside the job.

Now the other way round: historians generally say that by the early 1850s, easy-to-find surface gold was running out, and individual miners earned less. What would happen then to the cost of doing other jobs in San Francisco?

Try it: move "Go find gold" from low to high and watch the yellow box and the cost. Then move it below "Go back east for work". Where does the cost stop falling?
Exam-precise rule

When the value of the highest-valued option forgone increases (decreases), the opportunity cost increases (decreases).

In plain words: the cost is the value of the best option you gave up. If that value moves, the cost moves in the same direction.

Trap: If the best option forgone falls a lot, the cost does not always fall that far. If it falls below the third option, the third option becomes the new highest-valued option forgone. The cost only falls to the value of the third option. You can see this in the ladder above.
Situation 4

The value of another option forgone (not the best one) changes

StoryMarch 2024: the Eras Tour's only Southeast Asia stop

From 2 to 9 March 2024, Taylor Swift played six Eras Tour shows at the National Stadium in Singapore. It was the tour's only stop in Southeast Asia, and many fans from nearby places flew there to watch.

The student below is made up so we can do the numbers, but the concert dates and place are real. On a weekend in early March, the student has three options: stay in Hong Kong and study for Monday's mock exam (chosen), hike the MacLehose Trail with friends, or fly to Singapore for the Eras Tour (no ticket at first, so its value is very low).

Suddenly the student wins a concert ticket in a lucky draw. "Fly to Singapore for the concert" becomes more valuable. What happens to the opportunity cost of "studying in Hong Kong"?

"Fly to Singapore" was third, so it was not the cost. When it becomes more valuable, there are two possibilities. If it stays below "hiking", the best option forgone is still hiking, so the cost stays the same. If it goes above "hiking", it replaces hiking as the new highest-valued option forgone, so the cost increases. So your answer must state the condition. Try it in the ladder below.

Only "Fly to Singapore" can move. Push it up slowly. Watch when it passes "hiking", and what happens when it passes "studying".
Exam-precise rule

When the value of another option forgone changes, the opportunity cost increases only if that option replaces the original highest-valued option forgone.
Decrease → not affected. Increase → may increase, depending on whether the order of preference changes.

In plain words: if a lower option becomes worse, it can never become the best option given up, so the cost stays the same. If it becomes better, check whether it moves above the best option given up.

DSE wording trap: The question asks, "Will the opportunity cost necessarily remain unchanged?" If the cost may increase, the answer is No: "It does not necessarily remain unchanged." Many students see "may not change" and answer Yes. They lose all the marks.
Situation 5

The risk of something bad happening changes

StoryThe 2013 "killer pitch" at Hong Kong Stadium

In July 2013, Hong Kong Stadium held five international football matches in a few days, including the Barclays Asia Trophy. There was also very heavy rain. The grass turned into mud. Tottenham defender Jan Vertonghen hurt his ankle and was carried off on a stretcher. Sunderland manager Paolo Di Canio called it a "killer pitch".

Manchester United were due to play Kitchee there on 29 July. Manager David Moyes said in public that he was worried about the pitch. The team cancelled an open training session, and people feared the match might be cancelled. On the day, United checked the pitch, agreed to play, and won 5–2. After the match, the pitch was closed for repair until September, and the government department in charge had to review how it looked after the grass.

HKDSE 2014 Paper 1 Question 1 was based on this news.

(Adapted from HKDSE 2014 Paper 1 Q1) If Manchester United decide to play on this bad pitch, the opportunity cost of playing will ____, because ____.

Example only: the cost of playing = the best other option given up + the expected loss from injuries. Bar widths are not real numbers.

Exam-precise rule

When the risk of undesired events arising from choosing an option increases (decreases), the opportunity cost of choosing it increases (decreases), as the decision maker may have to give up more money or non-money resources (e.g., safety and health).

In plain words: if something bad is more likely to happen after you choose, you may lose more money, health or safety. So the cost goes up.

Putting it together

Build the summary table yourself

All five situations can be reduced to one question. Don't click yet. What is the question?

🎙️ — "Has what I give up to get my choice changed?" There are two kinds of things you give up. First, what you pay for your choice: money, time and risk. Second, the best option you cannot have because of your choice. If a change does not touch either of these, the cost does not change.

The order of questions to ask yourself in the exam

  1. First, find the option the question asks about, and the most valuable of the other options. The option asked about is not always the top choice.
  2. Does the change affect the value of the chosen option itself?
    → The cost stays the same. The benefit changes. (Situation 1)
  3. Does the change mean you pay more or less money, time or risk for the chosen option?
    → The cost changes in the same direction. (Situations 2 and 5)
  4. Does the change affect the highest-valued option forgone?
    → The cost changes in the same direction. If it falls below the third option, the cost only falls to the value of the third option. (Situation 3)
  5. Does the change affect another option forgone?
    → If it falls: no change. If it rises: check whether it passes the best option forgone, so the cost "does not necessarily remain unchanged". (Situation 4)
  6. Is the change about money or resources already used that cannot be got back?
    → This is a historical cost, not a cost of the decision. No change. (Textbook 1.3D, p.23. DSE MC questions often use this as a wrong option.)

Answer structure: "Explain whether the opportunity cost of X will change"

Based on the usual HKDSE marking logic, each part is often worth 2–3 marks, roughly like this:

StepWhat to writeMark
StateGive a clear conclusion: will increase / will decrease / will remain unchanged / does not necessarily remain unchanged (may increase)1
ExplainSay what the change affects: the value of the chosen option / money or time cost / the value of the highest-valued option forgone (name the option) / another option forgone / risk1
LinkLink back to the definition: because opportunity cost is the value of the highest-valued option forgone. For Situation 4, state the condition: "if it replaces …, then …"1 (3-mark questions)

Actual marks depend on the question and that year's marking scheme.
