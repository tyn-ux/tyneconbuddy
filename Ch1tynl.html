1 · Scarcity, Wants and Choice

Syllabus: Basic economic concepts — the source of all economic problems.

The worst drought in Hong Kong's recorded history. Reservoirs nearly empty, the government imposes water rationing (制水): at the worst point, tap water flows for only four hours every four days. Whole neighbourhoods queue in the street with buckets and kerosene tins; 「樓下閂水喉」 enters local folklore.

Water didn't vanish — there was still some. But the amount available could no longer satisfy what people wanted. That gap is the economist's precise meaning of scarcity — and the queues show what scarcity forces on us: choice, competition, and rules deciding who gets what.

Scarcity (缺乏) — tap to revealthe situation in which the resources available are not enough (insufficient) to satisfy all people's wants.

  • Economics — the study of how human beings allocate scarce resources to satisfy their unlimited wants. Micro: individuals' maximizing behaviour · Macro: aggregate phenomena.
  • Wants are unlimited — new wants come up when some are satisfied.
  • Scarcity is relative, not absolute: compare resources against wants. Limited resources alone ≠ scarcity.
  • Scarcity ⇒ choice: pick which wants to satisfy, which to give up. If a resource has only one use, no choice can be made.
  • "Limited resources ⇒ scarcity" — False. If limited resources can still satisfy all wants, there is no scarcity.
  • "Scarcity ⇒ there must be a market price" — False. Competition may be non-price (next section).

MC: Which statement is correct?

💡 B. Scarcity is a comparison of resources against wants — the classic True/False trap "limited resource ⇒ scarcity" is False, and competition criteria need not be price.

2 · Competition and Discrimination

Syllabus: relationship between scarcity, competition and discrimination.

At UCLA in the 1960s, economist Armen Alchian taught a way of seeing that travelled to Hong Kong through his student Steven N.S. Cheung (張五常), later a professor at HKU — who famously sold mandarin oranges at a Lunar New Year fair (《賣桔者言》, 1984) to watch pricing and competition happen live.

The insight: scarcity means not everyone gets the good, so some rule must decide who wins — price, queuing, exam results, lottery, connections. And every rule favours somebody and excludes somebody. There is no neutral rule.

Competition (競爭) — tap to revealthe process of resolving interpersonal conflicts over scarce goods — who can get the goods? Winners obtain them; losers do not.

Price competitionNon-price competition
BasisWilling and able to pay the market priceAge, qualifications, queuing, lottery…
ExampleArtworks sold at auction to the highest bidderSubsidised university places by exam results

Discrimination (歧視) — competition criteria by nature favour some group; those who cannot get the good are discriminated against by the rules of competition. Discrimination is always implied under competition.

Logic chain to memorise: Scarcity ⇒ competition ⇒ discrimination ✔ · Discrimination ⇒ scarcity ✔ · Market price ⇒ scarcity ✔ · Scarcity ⇒ market price ✘

Price discriminates against the poor — but rewards serving others' wants. Queuing "feels" fair but discriminates against those whose time is valuable, and burns hours. Lotteries are equal but reward nobody's effort. Economists disagree about which rule is better; they agree that no rule avoids discrimination — changing the rule only changes who is favoured. That is the Level 5** takeaway.

MC: A public hospital allocates specialist appointments by waiting list. Which is correct?

💡 C. First-come-first-served is non-price competition; it favours those able to wait and discriminates against those who cannot. "Discrimination" here is a technical description of any allocation rule, not a moral or legal accusation.

3 · Opportunity Cost

Syllabus: concept of opportunity cost — the single most-tested idea in this chapter.

For most of the 19th century, British economists thought cost meant the pain and toil of production — labour hours embodied in the thing. Then the Austrian school in Vienna — Friedrich von Wieser and colleagues, late 1800s — flipped it: the cost of anything is not the effort behind you, but the road not taken in front of you — the value of the best alternative you give up. (The English term "opportunity cost" was coined by David I. Green in 1894, building on the Austrian idea.)

Every DSE question about resale values, sunk payments and changing preferences tests whether you've made this mental switch.

Opportunity cost (機會成本) — tap to revealthe value of the highest-valued option forgone when a choice is made.

  • The next-best single alternative given up — not the sum of all alternatives forgone.
  • Arises from scarcity and choice; may have no relation to money. Money is only a unit for calculating cost.
  • No alternatives, or unlimited resources → opportunity cost does not exist.
  • The O.C. of the maximizing (first) choice is the lowest among all options — that's why a rational person picks it.

Micro-case: $10; ranking ① coke ② chocolate ③ fruit (each $10). Choose coke → O.C. = one bar of chocolate. Not "chocolate + fruit", not "$10".

  • O.C. ≠ money price — the $10 isn't the cost; the forgone chocolate is.
  • O.C. ≠ all alternatives added up — only the highest-valued forgone option.
  • "Free of charge" ≠ no opportunity cost (see §7, free goods).

💡 B — the single highest-valued option forgone. A is the "sum of all alternatives" trap; D is the "cost = money" trap.

4 · Will the Cost Change? — EE + HVOF

Syllabus: explain whether and how cost changes — asked in every single year 2012–2024.

Opportunity cost changes only when: ① the highest-valued option forgone (HVOF) itself changes (order rearranges), or ② the value of the HVOF changes. A change in the value of the chosen option never changes the cost.

DSE 2014 Q1(b): Serine's ranking: ① government AO ② accounting trainee ③ keep seeking a job. The government provides unemployment benefits to all graduates seeking jobs. Will her O.C. of choosing ① necessarily remain unchanged?

💡 B (official logic, 3 marks): benefit raises the 3rd option's value [EE] → it is possible the order rearranges → the HVOF may become "seeking a job with benefits". A is the exact trap "necessarily" is designed to catch; D is the opposite over-reaction.

DSE 2022 Q10f: Will the O.C. of being a professional athlete be affected if the average income of professional athletes increases?

💡 A — chosen-option value ↑ never touches the HVOF. B confuses value of the choice with the cost of the choice.

5 · Full Cost & Sunk Cost

Syllabus: explicit + implicit cost; why past irrecoverable expenditure is not a cost.

Nineteenth-century American saloons offered a "free lunch" with any drink — bread, cheese, salted meat. The lunch was priced into the drinks and into the hour you stood there; the saying "there ain't no such thing as a free lunch" grew from exactly this (Milton Friedman put it on a 1975 book cover). The cost of anything includes everything you give up — dollars and time.

And the opposite lesson: Britain and France kept funding Concorde through the 1960s–70s long after commercial failure was clear — partly because so much was already spent. Biologists Dawkins & Carlisle mocked this in 1976 as the "Concorde fallacy". Money already gone is not a cost of anything you do next.

Full cost — tap to revealthe highest-valued option forgone of the money/resources spent + the highest-valued option forgone of the time spent.

  • Explicit cost (外顯成本) — direct monetary payment (the ticket). Implicit cost (隱含成本) — value of resources given up with no direct payment (the time).
  • Time cost = the highest value of the time in its alternative use within that period — one job, never the sum of several.
  • Money is not itself a cost — only a unit used to calculate cost.
  • Sunk cost rule: irrecoverable past expenditure is not a cost; count only options available at the moment of decision.

Worked case: ① movie ($150, 2 hrs) ② KFC $40/hr ③ 7-Eleven $30/hr → time cost = 2 × $40 = $80 (KFC only). Full cost = HVOF of the $150 + $80 forgone KFC earnings.

DSE 2019 MC: car bought $60,000 (3 yrs ago), resale $2,000, repair $5,000 → cost of continuing to use = $5,000 + $2,000 = $7,000. The $60,000 is sunk.

  • Same queue ≠ same cost (DSE 2013 Q9c, MTR free-ticket queues): people have different alternative uses of time → different time costs → different full costs.
  • Holiday travel (DSE 2018 Q1): monetary cost higher, but income forgone (time cost) may be far lower — if the time-cost saving outweighs the extra monetary cost, holiday travel has the lower full cost.
  • Same family: free MOOC vs paid campus course (2015 Q1b) · weekend karaoke (SP Q4b) · queuing 10 hours vs paying touts (2023 Q11c — the housewife's time cost was low, so queuing was the cheaper full cost).

DSE 2021 Q4: Schools reopen after COVID online teaching. Which is the LEAST important factor for teachers choosing between online and face-to-face lessons?

💡 C — that time is a sunk cost: irrecoverable, identical whichever option is chosen now. A, B, D are live present/future costs and benefits.

6 · Interest — the Cost of Earlier Availability

Syllabus: interest as the cost of earlier availability of resources; MC regular.

Aristotle called interest unnatural — "money does not breed money." The medieval Church banned usury; Dante put usurers in the seventh circle of Hell. England legalized interest (capped at 10%) only in 1545 under Henry VIII.

In the 1880s, Austrian economist Böhm-Bawerk (Capital and Interest) reframed it: interest is the price of time. Present goods are worth more than identical future goods — we are impatient, the present is certain, resources now can produce now. Whoever gives up present availability deserves compensation; whoever gains earlier availability pays. That's interest — and it exists even with no money at all (lend a sack of rice, want a bit more rice back).

Interest (利息) — tap to revealthe cost of earlier availability of resources. Interest = interest rate × principal.

  • To the borrower: the cost paid for obtaining resources earlier (earlier consumption).
  • To the lender: the compensation received for deferring consumption.
  • Interest rate ↑ → cost of present consumption ↑ → present consumption ↓, saving ↑ (and vice versa).
  • Interest exists without money (barter) and without inflation; without scarcity, interest does not exist.

DSE 2012 Q3: Which statement about interest is correct?

💡 D — interest is the price of time, needing only scarcity and time preference; it survives barter, zero inflation, and any economic system. A reverses the direction; B confuses level with existence.

7 · Free Goods vs Economic Goods

Syllabus: definition and distinction — a 2-mark P2 template you can bank.

The free newspapers handed out at MTR exits cost zero dollars — yet the paper, ink, printing and distribution could all have produced something else, and some mornings the stack runs out before you reach it. The saloon "free lunch" all over again. The economist ignores the price tag and asks one question only: is the quantity available enough to satisfy all wants?

Free good (免費物品) — tap to revealquantity available is sufficient to satisfy all wants; no sacrifice needed for more; no opportunity cost. More of it is not preferred — no one will pay or give up resources for more.

Economic good (經濟物品) — tap to revealquantity available is insufficient to satisfy all wants; obtaining more involves sacrifice → opportunity cost exists; people compete for it.

  • The classification is situational: air is free on land, economic under the sea; river water free to the villager, scarce in the desert.
  • Free of charge ≠ free good. Full-mark template (2012 Q1 / 2020 Q1 / 2015 Q1a / 2023 Q1c): Producer view — the resources used have alternative uses, so something is forgone → O.C. exists. Consumer view — supply insufficient relative to demand; one person's possession deprives another.

DSE 2016 Q1: Which statement about economic goods is correct?

💡 B — insufficiency ⇒ competition. No one competes for what is sufficient for everyone (D), and the classification says nothing about preference (C).
🎙️ 過場 — 「免費 ≠ free good,睇夠唔夠」。最後兩個細位:circular flow / income vs wealth,同埋 positive vs normative。

8 · Economic Units, the Circular Flow, Income & Wealth

Syllabus: basic economic units and circular flow; flow vs stock.

François Quesnay was physician at the court of Louis XV at Versailles. Steeped in the discovery of blood circulation, he asked: does wealth circulate through an economy like blood through a body? His Tableau économique (1758) — a zigzag of payments between farmers, landlords and artisans — was the first circular-flow model, the direct ancestor of the diagram below.

  • Firm (廠商) — basic unit of production; decides what to produce and how to use resources.
  • Household (住戶) — basic unit of consumption; supplies resources (e.g. labour) to firms for income to finance consumption.
  • Income (收入) — total money and non-money return received over a period of time — a flow (salary $5,000 per month).
  • Wealth (財富) — total stock of assets owned at a point of time (on 31 Dec: savings $50,000 + car $80,000 = wealth $130,000).
Giveaway words: "per month / during the year" → flow → income · "on that day / at that moment" → stock → wealth.

9 · Positive vs Normative Statements

Syllabus: distinction between positive and normative statements — an MC every 2–3 years.

The Scottish philosopher David Hume noticed that writers slide mid-argument from what is to what ought to be — as if facts alone could tell you what to do. They can't; the leap needs a value judgement. A century and a half later, John Neville Keynes (father of Maynard) built the point into economics (The Scope and Method of Political Economy, 1891): keep positive economics (testable) apart from normative economics (value-laden).

Positive statement (實證性陳述) — tap to reveala statement that can be tested against facts (refutable by fact). It may be wrong — wrongness is testable too.

Normative statement (規範性陳述) — tap to reveala statement involving value judgements that cannot be tested against facts; about what should be / ought to be.

The test: "can facts reject it?" Yes → positive. Contains should / ought / better / too high / most appropriate → normative.
  • A false statement can still be positive — "unemployment is 40%" is testably wrong.
  • "Most people agree" is irrelevant (DSE 2021 Q1 distractor).
  • Predictions are positive: "the project can improve exam results" is refutable (DSE 2019 Q2a).

DSE 2021 Q1: Peter: "As a developed economy, Hong Kong's Gini coefficient is too high." Mary: "Hong Kong's Gini coefficient is the largest in Asia." Which is correct?

💡 C. "Too high" is a value standard (normative); "largest in Asia" is checkable against data (positive). Agreement (A) and factual accuracy (B) are both irrelevant to the classification.
